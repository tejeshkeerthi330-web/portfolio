Bitcoin: How the Industry Opened a Gateway to Criminal Activity

My first research project traced how the Silk Road — the Tor-only marketplace that made Bitcoin its payment backbone — shaped the currency's early price history. Step through the 2011–2013 arc below, station by station, exactly as the paper's event chart records it.

How the study was built

The method was document analysis: comparing accounts and price records across sources — RAND's work on terrorist use of cryptocurrencies, WIRED's reporting on the seizure, the Fordham Journal of Corporate & Financial Law, Investopedia's price histories — to find direct correlations between Silk Road events and Bitcoin's price movements.

What it found

  • Bitcoin rose from roughly $0.30 to ~$1,100 across the Silk Road's active years, 2011–2013.
  • At its peak, the marketplace accounted for roughly 20% of Bitcoin's economic activity, with total sales near 9.5 million BTC — about $1.2 billion.
  • Immediately after the FBI seizure, the price collapsed more than 81% from its pre-seizure high — the paper reads the two as directly related.
  • Across the full period, I inferred the Silk Road drove 5–15% of all Bitcoin price movements — a criminal marketplace as a measurable market force.

Limits, honestly

There is no fully accurate way to isolate one influence on a currency moved by countless factors — each can reinforce or mask the Silk Road's effect, so the 5–15% figure is an educated estimate, not a measurement. The paper sketches the isolation procedure a longer study would need: estimate and strip each competing influence, iterate, and accept that every estimate carries its own distortion.
