Topographical Effects of Materials on Aerodynamics

Does the surface finish of GT3 racecar bodywork matter aerodynamically? I ran seven CFD simulations — identical geometry, boundary conditions, and mesh, with only the wall roughness changed between runs — across the finishes a GT3 car actually wears. The answer: drag rose 124% from the smoothest finish to the roughest.

How the study was built

Average surface roughness values (Ra) for each material came from peer-reviewed literature and industry surface-finish specifications, converted to equivalent sand-grain roughness with the Nikuradse relation (ks ≈ 5.863 × Ra) — the wall roughness input CFD solvers expect.

The seven simulations ran steady-state and incompressible in SimScale with the k-ω SST turbulence model, the industry standard for wall-bounded flows. Identical geometry (a 100 mm sphere in a 2000 × 1000 × 1000 mm domain), identical boundary conditions (50 m/s inlet, 0 Pa gauge outlet, air at 293 K), identical mesh — only the wall roughness changed between runs, isolating surface topography as the sole variable.

What it found

  • Drag rose 124% from gloss carbon fiber (Cd 0.021) to matte vinyl wrap (Cd 0.047); skin friction followed, 0.0028 → 0.0065.
  • A clean performance threshold separates the finish groups: every gloss material stayed laminar or mostly laminar with Cd below 0.027; every matte material went transitional or turbulent with Cd above 0.034.
  • The mechanism is boundary layer transition: smooth surfaces held laminar flow to 78% of the plate; the roughest tripped it at 22%.
  • Within each group, ranking by roughness predicts ranking by drag — the magnitude of Ra, not the finish label, is the controlling variable.
  • For a GT3 team, the penalty is real: reduced top speed, higher fuel consumption, and diminished aerodynamic balance over a race distance.

Limits, honestly

Ra values were literature-sourced rather than measured from physical samples; the geometry was a simplified sphere, not a full GT3 body; SimScale's community-tier mesh introduces numerical error a research-grade mesh wouldn't; and steady-state runs can't capture transient behavior like vortex shedding. The paper closes with the questions these limits raise — from how roughness interacts with diffusers and wings to whether thermal cycling during a race degrades a finish's effective roughness.
